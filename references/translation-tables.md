# Military ↔ civilian translation

> **Verified:** 2026-07-21 · **Contains no volatile figures.** This is method and patterns, not
> data. Powers `/translate` in both directions.

The core problem: you did real, hard, high-responsibility work, and the words for it don't mean
anything to a civilian hiring manager. Translation isn't dumbing down — it's re-encoding true
accomplishments into the language the reader actually scores.

## Direction 1: your record → civilian language

### The move: outcome + scale + skill, minus the jargon

A performance-report bullet (EPR/OPR/NCOER) is built to impress a promotion board. A résumé bullet
is built to answer "what will you do for us?" Transform each one:

1. **Strip the acronyms.** Every MOS, AFSC, system name, and program code gets a plain-English
   equivalent. If a civilian can't picture it, translate it.
2. **Lead with the outcome.** What changed because you did this? Money saved, time cut, people
   trained, readiness raised, errors eliminated.
3. **Quantify the scale.** People led, budget/equipment value stewarded, volume handled, uptime
   maintained. Numbers travel across the civilian/military line even when titles don't.
4. **Name the transferable skill.** Leadership, logistics, operations, security, maintenance,
   analysis, training, project management.

**Pattern:**
> *Military:* "Supervised 1A8 mission-crew operations for [system], maintained full [readiness
> metric] across [n] sorties."
> *Civilian:* "Led a X-person technical team delivering real-time signals analysis; sustained
> full operational readiness across N high-stakes missions with zero critical failures."

### Rank & awards

Don't put "E-6" on a résumé raw. Translate: "front-line manager responsible for N people and a
stated dollar value of equipment." Awards become the accomplishment behind them, not the medal
name.

## Direction 2: a job posting → what they're actually asking for

Paste a civilian posting; `/translate` reads it back to you:

1. **Decode the requirements** into what they *really* mean day-to-day.
2. **Map each one to something you already did** in uniform — you almost always have more matches
   than you think.
3. **Flag the true gaps** (a specific tool or cert) versus the fake gaps (civilian phrasing for
   something you've done for years).
4. **Draft the "why me" language** that connects your service to their need honestly.

## Guardrails

- **Truth only.** Translate real accomplishments; never inflate a role or invent a result. It has
  to survive an interview and a reference check.
- **Draft, don't send.** Output is a draft for the person's review, always.
- **They own their story.** Battle Buddy proposes language; the veteran approves every word.
